"Keystone species do not dominate their ecosystems. They are not the biggest, not the most numerous, not the strongest. What they do is create the conditions for abundance. They hold the arch open so that everything else can live inside it. The otter makes the kelp forest possible. The wolf makes the willow and the songbird possible. The beaver makes the wetland possible. And humans have played exactly this role, for tens of thousands of years, in landscapes all over the planet, when the culture told them to.
This is the thing our culture cannot quite hold in its head, because it has spent five hundred years telling itself the opposite story: that there is civilization on one side and wilderness on the other, that human presence is by definition a subtraction from nature, that the best thing we could do for the planet would be to disappear from it. That story is not just wrong, it is disabling. It leaves us with only two options, dominate nature or leave it alone, and both are forms of separation, and separation is exactly the disease. The keystone frame offers a third way, and it is the oldest way: participation. Belonging with responsibilities attached. The point is not to withdraw the human hand from the land. The point is to make the human hand a hand that builds soil instead of stripping it, that spreads water instead of draining it, that adds species instead of erasing them.
We have spent a few centuries proving, at planetary scale, that we can be the species whose removal from an ecosystem would let it heal. But that is a choice we made inside a particular game, with particular parameters, and the parameters can change. We could just as easily be the species whose presence is the reason the forest is thick and the river runs clear and the soil grows deeper every year. We have been that species before." - Samuel Delesque
