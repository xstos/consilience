One mature tree cools the air as effectively as 10 room-size air conditioners running 20 hours a day — at zero energy cost, zero emissions, zero noise. 

That figure, from UCLA climate research, rests on two mechanisms: shade blocking solar radiation before it heats surfaces, and evapotranspiration releasing water vapour through leaf pores — nature's version of sweating, at canopy scale.

But a tree is also water infrastructure.

USGS research (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2021.151296) quantified that a single street tree intercepts roughly 6,400 litres of stormwater per growing season. Canopy, bark and stemflow slow rainfall and direct it into the root zone, where it infiltrates rather than runs off. Remove 31 street trees from a residential catchment — as documented in Wisconsin — and surface runoff increases by 198 cubic metres. Below ground, tree roots increase soil permeability and, through hydraulic lift, transfer deep moisture upward to surrounding shrubs and herbs with shallower roots, as confirmed in a 2026 study in Frontiers in Forests and Global Change (https://doi.org/10.3389/ffgc.2025.1671141). The tree waters its neighbours. It reduces irrigation demand for the entire plant community around it and sustains that community through drought.

Then the chain that planners still overlook.

A single native oak supports over 500 species of moths and butterflies, according to entomologist Douglas Tallamy at the University of Delaware, whose 2009 study in Conservation Biology (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1523-1739.2009.01202.x) established that native woody plants host far more caterpillar species than introduced ones — a finding independently confirmed in a 2020 Nature Communications study (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-19565-4) identifying oaks as North America's top keystone genus. Those caterpillars feed birds: a pair of chickadees requires 6,000 to 9,000 caterpillars to raise one clutch. A 2024 study in Landscape and Urban Planning (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169204624002731) found tree diversity drives insectivorous bird activity in cities — and those birds, in turn, control the pest insects that threaten the trees themselves. The tree feeds the insects, the insects feed the birds, the birds protect the tree, and the whole system moderates floods, drought and heat simultaneously.
